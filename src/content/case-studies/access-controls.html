01 / The context

Privacy is part of the conversation.

Port is a privacy-first messaging app. Access Controls extends that promise from the app to each individual connection, so people decide what every contact can do and see.

The problem

Nine controls, one small screen.

Per contact, people needed to manage six permissions and three chat settings. That is a lot of switches to put on a chat screen. Show them all and the conversation disappears under settings. Hide them all and the privacy promise goes with them. The real problem was never what the controls were. It was where they should live.

The design question
How might we make a rich set of contact permissions easy to discover, simple to manage, and quiet enough not to interrupt the conversation?
What needed to be controlled
Contact-specific permissions
  • Notifications and calls
  • Group additions and contact sharing
  • Read receipts and profile visibility
Chat settings
  • Home visibility and media downloads
  • Disappearing messages
02 / Design goals

Three tests every idea had to pass.

With a 24-hour window, there was no room to polish every direction. I turned the design question into three criteria so every concept could be judged against the same yardstick.

01

Easy to discover

A person should notice access controls exist without being told.

02

Simple to manage

Changing a permission should take a tap, and its current state should read at a glance.

03

Quiet

The control should never get between a person and their conversation.

03 / Exploration

Idea 1: A shortcut inside the chat.

The first direction put a floating icon in the conversation. Tapping it opens a compact permission panel; a close control returns you to the chat. For everything else, "View more settings" opens the full contact page.

What it offered

Quick access to permissions without leaving the conversation.

What it cost

Permissions were not immediately evident. The floating icon competed with the chat, and the full set of settings was still an extra step away.

What I took from it

Staying close to the conversation was right. A single floating icon was the wrong way to do it.

Idea 1: floating entry point and expanded contact settings.
04 / Exploration

Idea 2: Permissions in a horizontal scroll.

The second direction brought permissions into the chat as a horizontally scrollable row of icon pills. One tap on a pill turns a permission on or off, and the pill changes to show it: green when enabled, grey with a stroke through the icon when disabled.

The trade-off

Everything was visible at a glance. But the row would get crowded as permissions were added, and with no way to hide it, always-on controls risked becoming noise over time.

What I took from it

Visible, tappable state is the right language, and the grey-and-stroke "off" treatment carried into the final design. A permanent row did not.

Idea 2: horizontally browsable permission pills.
05 / Chosen direction

Idea 3: A slider that adapts to the moment.

The third direction resolved the tension between discoverability and distraction: a pull-down control in the chat header with three levels of visibility. The same space can be silent, glanceable or fully editable.

01

Fully closed

The conversation stays clear.

02

Semi-open

Icons provide a quick overview.

03

Fully open

Detailed permissions and toggles are available.

Idea 3: a three-state slider integrated into the chat header. Fully closed, semi open and fully open.
Refined direction

The slider, refined.

The slider carried forward with a more finished visual language. Every permission has its own coloured icon, "on" reads as green, and search, video and call stay in the header. The open panel tells people how to close it.

The refined slider in its three states.

06 / Interaction model

One control, every combination.

A row of icons only works if it reads correctly whatever the mix. Enabled permissions keep their colour; disabled ones turn grey with a stroke through them. I designed for the three states that matter.

All permissions enabled

Every icon is in colour. The contact has the full set.

All permissions disabled

Every icon is grey and struck through. No optional permissions are on.

A mix of permissions

Colour and grey sit side by side, so each permission reads and changes independently.

07 / Testing & refinement

The interaction still needed a cue.

A/B testing showed that some people did not realise the slider was interactive, or why it was there. A control built to stay quiet was, for some, easy to miss. The feedback pointed to an onboarding problem: the control needed to teach its own gesture.

What we saw

Some users did not understand they had to open the slider, or what it was for.

What it meant

The gap was discoverability: knowing the control exists and what it does. Not the permissions inside it.

What changed

Two cues, so the gesture teaches itself rather than relying on people to stumble onto it.

The two cues
01

First-time auto-open

Show the slider fully open when someone starts a chat with their first connection.

02

Micro-caption

"Drag this down to edit access permissions" makes the gesture explicit, right where the hand needs to go.

Status · Planned after testing
Refined interaction in context. The caption names the gesture.
08 / Outcome

Privacy, made practical.

Access Controls brought contact-specific permissions into the conversation itself, pairing a quick overview with a deeper settings view, without taking people away from their chat. The reported results are in the introduction above.

The design principle
Make control available when it is needed, and quiet when it is not.

The final direction balanced visibility, progressive disclosure and a low-interruption interaction, while keeping permissions close to the person and conversation they applied to.

What I took away.

A fast, collaborative exercise in turning a complex permission model into an interaction people could understand and control. Three lessons stayed with me.

01

Privacy needs depth and restraint

People want fine-grained control, but only when they reach for it. The rest of the time, it should stay out of sight.

02

A hidden control must teach its gesture

Quiet design earns its keep only if people can still find it. Testing made that very clear, very fast.

03

Write the trade-off down early

Naming what each concept offered and what it cost made a 24-hour decision a comparison, not a debate.

Access should feel like a choice — not an interruption.
